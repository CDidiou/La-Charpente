**My Mother :**
So, my little one. Tell me. What became of you?

**Me :**
I’m still the same, really, Mum. I love a celebration, a table laid full, glasses raised and laughter rising around me. But you knew me well enough to know that underneath it all there was always a quiet melancholy, a part of me that withdrew even in the middle of the noise. I’ve remained a man of my hands: I like things that are tangible, things I can mend, shape, make stand.

And I’ve never been able to bear seeing harmony crack, seeing people think only of themselves. It still wounds me, exactly as it did when I was a boy.

**My Mother :**
I remember you as a little boy, watching everything, sensing everything, wanting everyone to be happy around the table. And the family, after me? What became of it?

**Me :**
It carried on, Mum. Differently, but it carried on. For a long time, I think I carried something without knowing it. You were a strong woman, you filled a room, you decided things, gave orders now and then; and Dad, well, he was always trying to soften things, smooth the rough edges, make sure everyone stayed at peace. I watched him do it for years, and without realising it, I put on his clothes.

**My Mother :**
You mean you became like him.

**Me :**
Yes. I married a strong-minded woman, Mum. A woman with a mind of her own, who likes to organise, decide, move forward. And for a long time, I caught myself doing exactly what Dad had done with you: trying to calm her down, giving ground so the house would stay peaceful, forgetting myself a little so that the peace would hold. It took me a long time to understand that I was replaying a very old scene, one I had watched as a child without ever realising that it had taken up residence inside me.

**My Mother :**
And did you resent your father for being like that?

**Me :**
No, never. His joy and his bonds with other people were beautiful. He carried a quiet sadness — I could feel it — and for a long time I wanted to mend things for him, to mend something between the two of you that was never mine to mend. It took me years to understand that I wasn’t responsible for your marriage, Mum, and that I didn’t have to finish that particular battle in his place.

The day I understood that, I was finally able to set clearer boundaries in my own life without having to spend hours justifying myself.

**My Mother :**
And your marriage, then? How is life with your wife?

**Me :**
It’s grown up, Mum. She had an authoritarian father, and I think she hardened herself very young so that no one could ever crush her — partly to protect herself, partly to avenge her mother. She’s a woman who never backs down. And I came into her life with my hands held out, wanting to soothe, wanting to be gentle. I think she needed that: to prove that you can be strong and loved without having to crush anyone.

And I needed her to understand that I no longer had to charm or placate anyone to earn the right to exist beside them. We found our balance. I brought a lot of joy into the house. She kept the boat steady. We soon found our rhythm, each with our own territory, each respected, and both of us together.

**My Mother :**
And the children, my grandchildren? Tell me about them.

**Me :**
You know, we had two sons, Mum. The elder takes after his mother in many ways: a commanding temperament, someone who knows how to take his place, who builds companies rather than walls, who has never been afraid of difficult ground — technology, finance, territories I only half understand.

The other is his complete opposite, much more withdrawn, a mind that rises above things, watching the world from a great distance like an eagle on its crag, preferring to understand before he acts.

And neither of my boys is much of a practical man, compared with me! If Dad could see them, with their screens and their vocabulary, he wouldn’t recognise much of anything. But I think it’s less that they have changed than that the world around them has — people leaving the countryside, factories closing, craftsmanship eventually becoming something people thought was old-fashioned, a society more connected than ever and yet lonelier than ever.

For a long time I told myself I had passed nothing on, because neither of them resembles **Me :** not the parties, not the spontaneous warmth, not the need to gather everyone around a table.

**My Mother :**
And what do you think now?

**Me :**
I think I was wrong. I may not have given them my love of parties, but I gave them the ground beneath their feet, Mum. Security. The warmth of home. The certainty that their mother and I would always be there for them. Perhaps that is what allowed them to become builders, strategists, people who no longer need to bow to anyone. I look at them and think I didn’t fail in passing something on. I simply passed it on differently from how I imagined.

**My Mother :**
Do you feel you’ve found your place among all these people?

**Me :**
Yes, Mum. I finally have. I’m not the one who builds empires. But I’m the one who shows them the gesture, draws the plan properly, keeps the house warm, brings them together around a good meal, reminds them that beyond all their achievements and projects, there is a family that loves them.

I may sometimes be beaten when it comes to strategy, or action, but I’d rather let them win and not even know it. That isn’t what matters to me.

**My Mother :**
And our house? What did you do with it, you and your sister?

**Me :**
That’s just it. Do you remember that old book, Mum? The one we found in the old chest in the attic when I was little. You used to read it to me sometimes, in the evenings. We never knew whether it had belonged to one of our ancestors or to the people who had lived in the house before us. A man talking about carpentry, family, war… It didn’t make much sense. We smiled. It felt a bit like a legend.

**My Mother :**
Yes, I remember. You loved hearing me tell you that story. You listened without quite understanding, and eventually you’d fall asleep.

**Me :**
I promise you, Mum, I’m not going to sell this house. I’ll restore it. Even if it takes time. Even if my sons don’t understand it straight away. There are some things you don’t simply let fall. Some roof frames have to be raised again, even when you’re no longer quite sure who you’re doing it for.

**My Mother :**
You’ve done beautiful work, my son.

**Me :**
I did what I could, Mum, with what you and Dad gave me.
