**My mother:**
So, my little one. Tell me about it. What have you become?

**Me:**
I'm still the same, deep down, mom. I love the party, the full table, the clinking glasses and the rising laughter. But you knew me well enough to know that underneath, there has always been a quiet melancholy about me, a part of me that withdraws even amid the noise. I have remained a man of my hands: I like what is concrete, what I can repair, shape, make stand up. And I could never stand to see harmony crumble, to see people only thinking about themselves. It still affects me like a wound, just like when I was little.

**My mother:**
I remember you, a little boy, who looked at everything, who smelled everything, who wanted everyone to be comfortable at the table. And the family, after me? What happened to her?

**Me:**
She continued to live, Mom. Otherwise, but she continued. For a long time, I think I carried something without knowing it. You were a woman of character, you took up all the space in a room, you decided, you commanded a little, and Dad always tried to soften things, to round out the corners, to ensure that everyone remained in peace. I watched him do it for years, and without realizing it, I took his clothes.

**My mother:**
You mean you did like him.

**Me:**
Yes. I married a woman of character, Mom. A strong-willed woman who likes to organize, decide, and move forward. And for a long time, I found myself doing exactly what Dad did with you: trying to appease him, giving ground so that the house would remain calm, forgetting about myself a little so that the peace would hold. It took me a while to understand that I was reenacting a very old scene, a scene that I had seen as a child without ever suspecting that it had taken root in me.

**My mother:**
And you were mad at him, at your father, for being like that?

**Me:**
No, never. His joy and connections with others were magnificent. He carried a discreet sadness, I felt it, and for a long time I wanted to repair for him, to repair between you two something that I did not have to repair. It took me years to understand that I was not responsible for your relationship, Mom, that I did not have to finish this fight for her. The day I understood this, I was finally able to set clearer limits in my own life, without needing to justify myself for hours.

**My mother:**
What about your marriage then? How is your life with your wife?

**Me:**
It's matured, mom. She had an authoritarian father, and I believe that she had hardened herself very young to never let herself be crushed, a little to defend herself, a little to avenge her mother. She is a woman who will stop at nothing. And I arrived with my hands outstretched, my desire to soothe, my gentleness. I think she needed that: to prove that you can be strong and loved without having to crush anyone. And I needed her to understand that I no longer had to coax anyone to have the right to exist alongside her. We found our balance, I inspired a lot of joy. She held the boat. We quickly found a balance, each with their own territory, each respected, and both together!

**My mother:**
And the children, my grandchildren? Tell me about them.

**Me:**
You know, we had two sons, mom. The eldest takes a lot from his mother: a commander's temperament, someone who takes his place, who builds companies rather than walls, who is never afraid to advance in difficult terrain - tech, finance, territories that I only half understand. The other is quite the opposite, completely withdrawn, a mind which gains height, which observes the world from very far away like an eagle on its rock, which prefers to understand before acting. And not very manual, my sons, compared to me! If Dad saw them, with their screens and their vocabularies, he wouldn't recognize much. But I believe that it is less them who have changed than the world around them - the rural exodus, the factories which have closed, the crafts which we ended up finding out of date, this society which is so connected and yet more alone than ever. For a long time I told myself that I had transmitted nothing, because neither of them resembles me: neither the party, nor the spontaneous warmth, nor the need to bring everyone together around a table.

**My mother:**
And now what do you think?**Me:**
I think I was wrong. I may not have given them my taste for partying, but I gave them the ground beneath their feet, Mom. The security, the warmth of home, the certainty that we would always be there for them, me and my wife. Perhaps it is thanks to this that they were able to become builders, strategists, people who no longer need to bow to anyone. I look at them and tell myself that I didn't mess up my transmission, I just gave it differently than I imagined.

**My mother:**
Do you find your place among all these people?

**Me:**
Yes, mom, I finally found it. I'm not the one who builds empires. But I am the one who shows the gesture, really draws the plan, keeps the house warm, the one who still brings them together around a good meal, the one who reminds them that beyond the successes and projects, there is a family that loves each other. I may sometimes be beaten in strategy, or in action, but I prefer to let them win and not really know, that's not what interests me.

**My mother:**
And our house, what did you and your sister do with it?

**Me:**
Exactly. Do you remember this old book, Mom? The one we found in the old chest in the attic when I was little. You read it to me in the evening, sometimes. We never knew if it came from one of our ancestors or from the people who lived in the house before us. A man who talked about carpentry, family, war... It didn't make much sense. We smiled. It was a bit of a legend.

**My mother:**
Yes, I remember. You loved it when I told you this story. You listened without really understanding, and you ended up falling asleep.

**Me:**
I promise you, mom, I won't sell this house. I'll retype it. Even if it must take time. Even if my sons don't understand right away. There are things we don't let go of. Frameworks that we must raise, even when we no longer really know for whom.

**My mother:**
You did a great job, my son.

**Me:**
I did what I could, Mom, with what you and Dad gave me.
