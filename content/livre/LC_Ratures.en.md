*(Written on the last page of the book, in lively handwriting, in blue pen.)*

You messed up on mom.
You wrote that she gave you the keys and forbade you to open the door. It's pretty. This is false.
She didn't give you anything on purpose. She lived in it, that's all. Her files, her children's stories, her anecdotes... she didn't even realize that you were taking. Because you knew how to disappear. You've been acting like a chameleon since you were six. A normal kid, she would have seen him straight away. You don't. You hung out where she wasn't looking, you listened, you stored, and you kept your mouth shut. You stole the keys. And then you took to engineering like a good little soldier, so that no one would suspect that you already had them in your pocket. She wasn't the one who blocked you. It was you who preferred to obey rather than take responsibility for what you had done in silence.

And the rest is the same.

Mom, she turns the tap on full blast. All the time. Always.
Do you remember summers at the sea? She had prepared three different snacks because one of us didn't like ham, the other wanted cheese, and she hadn't eaten anything herself.
Do you remember Christmases? She did the shopping, the meal, the decor, the beds, the reception, the dishes... everything. You got up at eight o'clock to see if you had any presents under the tree, and she had been there for at least two hours, she had made you coffee and washed and ironed your shirt so that you left in the evening with a suitcase cleaner than when you arrived!

Do you remember your license blocked at the prefecture? Six months of hitchhiking to work, six months of telling yourself that the file would eventually come out on its own. She made a connection with a secretary at the counter, she returned there twice with flowers, without telling you, and your file was cleared in a week. She never told you how she did it. She just sorted it out, behind the scenes, without anyone asking her.

And do you remember the summer when I found myself without anyone to watch the little ones? She drove two hours every Wednesday, all summer, to cover for me. And in addition to babysitting, she took the opportunity to clean the apartment from top to bottom and stock the fridge before leaving — as if taking care of the children wasn't already enough of a service provided.

She doesn't dose. She never dosed. She gives as if she had to make up for something she didn't have when she was little. And every time she heals someone, she heals a little bit herself. You took it as science that she was passing on to you. No. It was just the tap wide open. Point.

Dad, it's the same, the other way around.
He grew up with the sound of the plane which was supposed to silence the rest. So as soon as he could, he did the opposite: he filled it.

Do you remember Sunday meals? Twelve at the table for eight seats provided, and he who brought back a bottle from the cellar as soon as a glass was below half, before anyone even had time to ask him for it. Nobody was thirsty, really. He still served.

Do you remember the party committee at work? Nobody had asked him anything. He volunteered on his own, year after year. The barbecue was him. The group's trip was still him - the quotes, the buses, the visit program, everything.

And do you remember that weekend when he drove seven hours to change two faucet tips and an electric domino at your house at college? Seven o'clock. For that. And he would have come back if necessary eight days later, you know that very well.

And all these acquaintances who regularly showed up with a piece of land and an idea for a house, remember? He made the plans for them, free of charge, for the building permit file. Entire evenings hunched over the table drawing facades for people we barely knew. Never a cent asked. Never asked for a thank you. Just the desire for things to move forward, for someone else to also have a place to come back to.


Two taps open.
Two parents who give without gauge.

And we, in the middle, have to learn alone not to drown.
You and I just chose different directions to evacuate the excess.
I'm going out. I charge forward, I order, I welcome everyone before they invite me, I get angry as soon as things slow down. I need resistance to feel that I exist.

You go back. You observe, you tidy up, you make theories even in the middle of a crisis. You burn in a closed circuit.
We had too much fuel and not enough oxygen.

You talked about a house where everyone is calculating.
Whereas it was just a house where everyone gave too much, without ever looking at the meter.

That's all.
Your book is beautiful. It is false.
