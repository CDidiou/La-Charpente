# Conclusion?

## What I wanted to say, by writing this family that I didn't have

I will try to explain, once, what I tried to do with these four. Not because a book needs to be explained, but because this one, I believe, helped me understand something that the war and the return had never given me time to understand.

Taken one by one, these four portraits tell the story of four fairly clear determinisms. The father repairs, because he grew up having to repair what was not his place to repair. The mother commands and protects, because she grew up as an issue rather than as a loved one. The eldest rushes forward, because one of the two sons had to take the place that the other left empty. The cadet observes and doubts, for the same reason, in a mirror. None of the four has chosen their starting square. I know this sentence better than anyone: I didn't choose mine either, the youngest of siblings too numerous for the earth, leaving before having had time to know if I would have stayed.

And yet, none of these four characters, taken in isolation, could ever have pivoted alone. This, I believe, is the heart of what I wanted to write — because that is exactly what I missed. In war, there are many of us, piled on top of each other, and yet we are alone there as we never are elsewhere: each in his own hole, with his own fear, which no one else can carry in his place. We come away convinced that we must face everything alone, since that is what we have just done for months. In any case, that's what I learned from it, and it cost me dearly. The youngest son in this book only allows himself to use his gaze in front of his mother, who gives it to him. The father only makes peace with the lost cousins ​​thanks to his sons, then thanks to his wife, who ends up telling him everything. The eldest only understands his own stubbornness while holding a tool next to his father, in a house that neither of them would have repaired on their own. I wanted, through them, to write what I never knew how to do: repair together, rather than coming back from a trench with the idea that solitude is a virtue.

This is what gives its exact meaning to the image of the table being held. The father of this book is not the least determined character of the four — he is perhaps, on the contrary, the most confined in his function, the one who has been repairing since childhood without ever having really chosen this role. But it is precisely because he holds this fixed position, without ever deserting it, that the other three had a stable place from which to dare to move. I never had this table. I returned to a house where everyone was tearing themselves apart over a boundary, an inheritance, an old grudge that no one knew how to tell in order anymore — and there was no one to hold anything steady while the others around could have learned to move without getting lost. So I wrote the table that I didn't get. A table that never moves is not a lack of ambition: it is the infrastructure that allows all the rest of the family movement to exist without getting lost. I wanted this table to be solid, just as I would have liked, when I came home, to find someone who had not moved while I was away fighting for a dream which had slipped away.

True freedom does not reside deep within the individual — it resides in the quality of the bond. My family that I wanted is the one that has this capacity, dialogue after dialogue, to tell each other's stories, to say what each person has carried alone until then. It's perhaps naive to write that, at my age, with what I've seen. But I believe that it is precisely because I have never experienced it that I needed to write it so precisely. Nothing, in this trajectory that I invented, claims that an individual has ever freed himself from what preceded him. What the trajectory tells is more modest and truer than what I experienced myself: that the only space where determinism can, in places, give up a little ground, is the space between two people who agree to talk to each other, not the interior space of just one. I didn't have that space. I had one leg missing and a silence that no one ever came to break for me.This is why the last image of this book is neither a single man nor a single woman who has finally unraveled her own mystery, but a table, with four chairs, where everyone remains who they are - the strategist, the lioness, the builder, the observer - and where it is precisely from remaining seated together, year after year, despite everything that determines them separately, that comes the only form of freedom that this family will ever truly know. I never had that table. But I wrote it, as solidly as I could, for someone else.
