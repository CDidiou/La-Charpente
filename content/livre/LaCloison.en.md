# The House we are renovating
*Imaginary dialogue between father and eldest son*

---

**The eldest son:**
It feels weird putting your hands back in there. It's crazy how small the walls look now.

**Father:**
They didn't shrink, you grew. Here, give me the crowbar, we'll see what's behind this partition.

**The eldest son:**
Do you remember anything in particular in this room?

**Father:**
Your grandfather worked right there, under the window. He had set up his workbench in this precise spot because the morning light fell on it. I watched him for hours, without him asking me anything. He didn't talk much, your grandfather. But when the house rumbled a little too much, because your grandmother was in charge, he would come down here, take a piece of wood, and let the sound of the plane silence the rest.

**The eldest son:**
He was fleeing.

**Father:**
Here, yes. But he wasn't just that, you know. Outside, in the village, he was another man. He went out drinking with the others, he sang out of tune and loudly, he organized neighborhood parties, everyone loved him, everyone looked for him to host a party. Two men under the same hat, in fact: the one who was silent here, in this workbench, and the one who was shining outside, in the street.

**The eldest son:**
And you, which of the two did you take?

**Father:**
Both, without meaning to. I wanted to become the man of the village. Consciously, actively. The party, the warmth, the gathering, that's what I saw of him on the outside, and that's what I wanted to become. But inside, without me realizing it, I kept the other. The one who is silent, who absorbs, who lets the storm pass rather than responding to it. I took my father's public image as a model, and his private character as a reflex.

**The eldest son:**
It's a strange sharing.

**Father:**
For a long time I thought it was weakness, this withdrawal he had here, in this room. Today I think it was another, quieter form of force. She commanded, he stabilized — in the house. And outside he gathered. I just merged the two places into one man, without distinguishing, without even knowing that I was doing it.

**The eldest son:**
It's funny you say that. Because I, as a kid, thought the same thing about you — but I only had the version of you inside the house. I only saw the man giving in.

**Father:**
It's possible. A child especially sees what happens under his own roof. The rest we have to tell him.

**The eldest son:**
I saw and believed that you gave in all the time. That mom decided everything and that you followed, smiling. I told myself that I really didn't want to become like that. I wanted to order, not adapt.

**Father:**
And you did exactly what you wanted. You have become someone who commands.

**The eldest son:**
Yes. And now that I'm carrying the crowbar for you, I realize that I don't know if I was right.

**Father:**
Explain to me.

**The eldest son:**
I wanted to be the opposite of you. Structured, tough in negotiation, never dismantled. And I succeeded, in a sense: I built, I moved forward, I never needed to be appeased. But my sons, I have the impression that they sometimes look at me as if I was someone who was tiring to live with. Like me, I looked at you before, thinking that you were giving in too much.

**Father:**
In short, they would think the exact opposite of what you thought of me!

**The eldest son:**
There you go. I wanted to be your opposite because you gave in too much, in my eyes. And maybe they will want to be my opposite because I command too much. Dad, now that I think about it, I tell myself that we each made the same gesture, but in opposite directions.

**Father:**
That's what becoming a man is all about, I think. We start by defining ourselves against our father. We take the opposite path to ours, we are sure we are right, we move forward. And then one day, without warning, we finally understand what the father was trying to do. We say to ourselves: ah, so that was it. We would like to tell him, sometimes he is no longer there to hear it. And at the exact moment when we understand this, our own son comes to tell us that we are wrong.

**The eldest son:**
By the time a man realizes that his father was right, he already has a son who tells him he is wrong.

**Father:**
That's exactly it.

**The eldest son:**
I can't believe it.**Father:**
But it also means that your son, one day, will in turn understand what you were trying to do for him. It's not a failure, this story, it's just the tempo. We cannot be understood immediately by the one we are raising. We didn't understand it either.

**The eldest son:**
Were you afraid of transmitting incorrectly?

**Father:**
All the time. I didn't have your builder's energy, nor did I have my father's silence. I made a mixture, the celebration and the worry underneath. For a long time I told myself that I had nothing solid to give you and your brother, because I wasn't building anything big, I was just holding the table.

**The eldest son:**
You gave me more than you think.

**Father:**
Being a father, I believe, is not about passing on what is solid in us. It's transmitting the place to come back to when things shake. Your grandfather had this workbench. I had this table, where everyone gathered. What do you give them, your sons, as a place to return to?

**The eldest son:**
I don't know yet. I think above all I gave them a direction, a direction to follow. Not necessarily a place to settle down.

**Father:**
It's not too late to add the location.

**The eldest son:**
What are you thinking about?

**Father:**
To that, precisely. My house. If we renovate it together, you and I, maybe it will become a place where your sons can come, without needing to rush or understand anything before entering. Just a place waiting for them.

**The eldest son:**
A workbench under a window. The artisan's refuge.

**Father:**
Not a workbench. A large table. To bring together.

**The eldest son:**
I think I understand a little better now what you were doing when you gave in in front of Mom. You weren't really giving in. You waited for the storm to pass, and you kept the house warm during that time.

**Father:**
There you go. And you, when you command too much with your sons, you don't really command either, I imagine. You're afraid they'll bump into each other, so you make the path for them as quickly as possible.

**The eldest son:**
No doubt. I had never thought of it like that.

**Father:**
That's the main danger, with the wires. We think we are watching them, when we are mainly watching ourselves, younger, making the same mistakes as them.

**The eldest son:**
So what do we do now with this partition?

**Father:**
We take it away. She wasn't wearing anything, anyway, your grandfather had put it together just to separate two rooms that didn't need to be. A bit like the two of us before today.

**The eldest son:**
Dad, tell me honestly: do you think I was right, to become the complete opposite of you?

**Father:**
I think you were right to try. And I think that real success is not having been right against me. It's about being here today, fixing up this house together, without either you or I having to be right anymore.

**The eldest son:**
So we remove this partition.

**Father:**
We take it away.
