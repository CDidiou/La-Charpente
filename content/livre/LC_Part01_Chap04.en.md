**The Elder Son :**
It feels strange, putting my hands to this again. It’s mad how small the walls seem now.

**The Father :**
They haven’t shrunk. You’ve grown. Here, pass me the crowbar. Let’s see what’s behind this partition.

**The Elder Son :**
Do you remember anything in particular about this room?

**The Father :**
Your grandfather used to work right here, under the window. He set up his workbench in exactly this spot because the morning light fell on it. I’d watch him for hours, without him ever asking me for anything. Your grandfather didn’t say much. But when the house got a little too loud, because your grandmother ran everything with an iron hand, he’d come down here, pick up a piece of wood, and let the sound of the plane drown out the rest.

**The Elder Son :**
He was escaping.

**The Father :**
Here, yes. But that wasn’t all he was, you know. Out in the village, he was a different man. He’d go for a drink with the others, sing loudly and badly, organise the village fêtes. Everyone liked him, everyone looked for him when there was a party to liven up. Two men under the same hat, really: the one who kept quiet here, at his workbench, and the one who shone out there, in the street.

**The Elder Son :**
And which one did you become?

**The Father :**
Both, without meaning to. I wanted to become the village man. Consciously, deliberately. The celebrations, the warmth, everyone coming together — that was what I saw in him outside, and that was what I wanted to become. But inside, without realising it, I kept the other one. The one who keeps quiet, absorbs everything, lets the storm pass rather than answering it. I took my father’s public image as my model, and his private nature as my reflex.

**The Elder Son :**
That’s a strange division.

**The Father :**
For a long time I thought it was weakness, that withdrawal of his in this room. Now I think it was another kind of strength, a quieter one. She gave the orders, he kept things steady — in the house. And outside, he brought people together. I simply fused the two places into one man, without distinguishing between them, without even knowing I was doing it.

**The Elder Son :**
It’s funny you should say that. Because when I was a boy, I thought the same thing about you — but I only knew the version of you inside the house. All I saw was the man who gave way.

**The Father :**
That’s possible. A child mostly sees what happens under his own roof. The rest has to be told to him.

**The Elder Son :**
I saw you, and I thought you gave way all the time. Mum decided everything and you just followed along, smiling. I told myself I never wanted to become like that. I wanted to be the one in charge, not the one who adapted.

**The Father :**
And you did exactly what you wanted. You became someone who gives the orders.

**The Elder Son :**
Yes. And now that I’m holding the crowbar instead of you, I’m beginning to wonder whether I was right.

**The Father :**
Tell me.

**The Elder Son :**
I wanted to be your opposite. Structured, tough in negotiations, never fazed. And in a way, I succeeded: I built things, I moved forward, I never needed anyone to soothe me. But sometimes I feel my sons look at me as though I’m exhausting to live with. Just as I used to look at you, thinking you gave way too easily.

**The Father :**
So, in the end, they’ll think the exact opposite of what you thought about me!

**The Elder Son :**
Exactly. I wanted to be your opposite because, in my eyes, you gave way too much. And perhaps they’ll want to be my opposite because I give too many orders. Dad, now that I think about it, we’ve each made the same move, just in opposite directions.

**The Father :**
I think that’s what becoming a man is. You start by defining yourself against your father. You take the road opposite to his, certain you’re right, and you carry on. And then one day, without warning, you finally understand what your father was trying to do. You think: Ah. So that was it. Sometimes you want to tell him, but he’s no longer there to hear you. And at the exact moment you understand, your own son comes along and tells you you’re wrong.

**The Elder Son :**
By the time a man understands that his father was right, he already has a son telling him he’s wrong.

**The Father :**
Exactly.

**The Elder Son :**
I can’t believe it.

**The Father :**
But it also means that one day your son will understand what you were trying to do for him. This story isn’t a failure. It’s just a matter of timing. The person you’re raising can’t understand you straight away. We didn’t understand our fathers either.

**The Elder Son :**
Were you ever afraid you’d pass the wrong things on?

**The Father :**
All the time. I didn’t have your builder’s energy, and I didn’t have my father’s silence either. I made a mixture of the two: the celebrations, with worry underneath. For a long time I told myself I had nothing solid to give you and your brother, because I wasn’t building anything grand. I was only keeping the table standing.

**The Elder Son :**
You gave me more than you think.

**The Father :**
I think being a father isn’t about passing on whatever is solid in us. It’s about passing on the place to come back to when everything starts to shake. Your grandfather had this workbench. I had this table, where everyone came together. Look at that graffiti there, on the side. ZP and PBL inside a heart. I’ve always seen it there, unchanged. My parents used to laugh and tell me that, when they were young, they called each other the Zestful Princess and the Precious Beloved Lad. You see, I’ve always been fond of that graffiti.

And you? What are you giving your sons as a place to come back to?

**The Elder Son :**
I don’t know yet. I think what I’ve given them, mostly, is a bearing. A direction to follow. Not necessarily somewhere to put their feet down.

**The Father :**
It’s not too late to add the place.

**The Elder Son :**
What are you thinking of?

**The Father :**
This, exactly. My childhood home, the one I inherited with my sister. If you and I restore it together, perhaps it can become somewhere your sons can come without needing to rush anywhere or understand anything before they walk through the door. Just somewhere that is waiting for them.

**The Elder Son :**
A workbench under a window. The craftsman’s refuge.

**The Father :**
Not a workbench. A big table. A place to bring people together.

**The Elder Son :**
I think I understand a little better now what you were doing when you gave way to Mum. You weren’t really giving way. You were waiting for the storm to pass, and keeping the house warm while it did.

**The Father :**
That’s it. And you, when you order your sons around too much, you’re not really giving orders either, I suppose. You’re afraid they’ll hurt themselves, so you draw the quickest possible path for them.

**The Elder Son :**
Probably. I’d never thought of it like that.

**The Father :**
That’s the great danger with sons. You think you’re watching them, when really you’re mostly watching yourself, younger, making the same mistakes they are.

**The Elder Son :**
So what do we do now, with this partition?

**The Father :**
We take it down. It wasn’t holding anything up anyway. Your grandfather put it in simply to divide two rooms that never needed dividing. A bit like the two of us, before today.

**The Elder Son :**
Dad, tell me honestly: do you think I was right to become your complete opposite?

**The Father :**
I think you were right to try. And I think true success isn’t proving you were right against me. It’s being here, today, restoring this house together, without either of us needing to be right any more.

**The Elder Son :**
Then let’s take the partition down.

**The Father :**
Let’s take it down.
