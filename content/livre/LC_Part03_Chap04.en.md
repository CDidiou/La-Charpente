*(Childhood home. The new shutters are open. The light comes in too well. A man in a dark suit is already in the living room, a file under his arm, an employee behind him with a notebook. The mother is standing near the large table, her hands placed flat on the wood. She has not moved since they entered.)*

**The bailiff:**
Madam, I will have to proceed. You have received the notifications.

**The mother:**
Yes.

**The bailiff:**
We start with the furniture in this room. The table, the chairs, the sideboard. Then upstairs.

*(She doesn't answer. She looks at the table. The employee is already writing down.)*

**The bailiff:**
Is anyone else present? One of your sons, perhaps?

**The mother:**
No.

**The bailiff:**
Good. We then move forward.

*(He makes a sign. The employee approaches the buffet, opens the doors, takes out the plates one by one, places them in open boxes. The sound of the porcelain is dry, precise. The mother follows each gesture with her eyes, without intervening.)*

**The bailiff:**
You can keep personal belongings. Clothes, papers, small souvenirs. The rest is inventoried.

**The mother:**
I know.

*(A silence. We hear the employee going upstairs. Footsteps above their heads. A creaking door. The mother remains still.)*

**The bailiff:**
Madam… are you okay?

**The mother:**
No.

*(She says this without dramatizing. Just an observation. Then she removes her hands from the table, letting them hang along her body. She sits heavily on the only chair that has not yet been moved. Her shoulders sag. For the first time in years, she no longer organizes anything, no longer decides anything, no longer protects anything.)*

**The mother:**
Do.

*(The bailiff looks at her for a second too long, then resumes his work. He passes through the corridor, stops in front of the front door. The raw wooden sign is still hanging outside, discreetly: “Guest rooms”. He detaches it with a clear gesture, turns it over, places it against the wall, invisible text side.)*

**The bailiff:**
We also take this one. It is one of the signage elements linked to the activity.

*(The mother does not look up. She stares at a point on the ground, between her feet. We hear the dull noise of the chairs being stacked outside. Then the sideboard being moved. The house is emptied in pieces, without haste, with the boring precision of official things. The employee finally comes back downstairs with the chest from the attic.)*

**The bailiff:**
This old chest is very heavy!

*(Suddenly a gust of wind. A door slams. The usher, surprised, almost falls down the stairs, with his solid wooden chest in his hands. An old parchment escapes. It rolls to the mother's feet. Slowly she picks it up. She opens it. Her eyes can no longer read. The parchment slips from her hands.)*

**The bailiff:**
We'll come back tomorrow for the rest. Kitchen, bedrooms. Will you be there?

**The mother:**
I don't know.

*(He notes something, hesitates, then leaves. The door remains open for a moment. We see the sign placed against the wall, face down. A draft causes it to tip slightly. It does not get up.)*

*(The mother is still sitting. She no longer has the energy to get up, or to close the door, or to look at what is being taken away. She remains there, in the middle of the too-empty room, her hands limp on her knees, like someone who has finally stopped holding on.)*

*It’s the wind from outside that comes to remind us of what wood we are really made of.*


## The parchment, a letter probably from Joseph

To those who will come,

I came home with one leg missing, a fixed idea, and nothing else. A table. Voices. A place to come back to. I said it to anyone who would listen. It didn't last, and it didn't last because of me.

She was waiting for me. I couldn't stay. I always had one more piece of land to buy, one more piece of wood to cut, one less leg and yet never had the time to sit across from anyone. I confused building with loving. I thought filling a barn was enough to fill a house.

She left without fanfare, because there was nothing to dispute: I had given nothing that could be taken back from me.

I didn't have children. I had no one in the evening to explain a frame to. I calculated beams all by myself, I erected empty houses for people I would never see again, and I ended up writing, I who had never known how to speak, a family who knew how to talk to each other. It wasn't mine. I've never had one.

Sisyphus, at least, has his stone. I only had the gesture, never the weight of something to lose.

I didn't start a home. I didn't leave anyone behind. I leave these pages like you leave a beam that you never knew how to put: we'll see if anyone wants them.*(Here the writing changes. Tighter, more tilted, like a hand that trembled then recovered by force.)*

See, Joseph, what I write about you for eternity. You will never read these lines. They will read them for you.
MZ.
