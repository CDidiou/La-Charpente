Two months later.
The father came home. Not at the childhood home (partially sold, transformed, lost). In a smaller apartment, on the ground floor, with a ramp and a large window overlooking a square. The mother comes there three times a week. She brings the groceries, tidies up a bit, sits for an hour, leaves. She doesn't decide much anymore. She's there, that's all.
That day, the eldest came by to get the bank papers. He stood, spoke quickly, spoke again. Then the cadet arrived. He didn't bring anything. He just sat down.
The father is in his armchair near the window. He looks at the children playing in the square. From time to time he smiles, for no specific reason.

**The father:**
Do you remember my father’s workbench under the window?

**2D:**
Yes.

**The father:**
Your grandfather stayed for hours. He didn't say anything. I stayed behind, watching him.
It's funny. Now I'm the one looking out the window. And you behind.

The cadet does not respond immediately. He also looks at the square.

**Tazar:**
Did mom come by this morning?

**The father:**
Yes. She left some fruit. She said she would come back on Thursday.

She doesn't stay long anymore. But she's coming.
Silence.

**The father:**
Do you have a lot of things to sort out?

**Tazar:**
Yes. I'm the one who takes care of it.





There are three of them in front of the large window. The father in his armchair, a little floating, his eyes half-closed at times. The eldest standing, hands in his pockets. The cadet on the chair a little too low, next to it.
Outside, a child is screaming. A ball bounces against the fence. The father smiles again, very lightly.

The father, without looking at them.

**The father:**
You guys sucked.

The elder turned his head. The younger one too.

**The father:**
You rushed in like an idiot.
You looked at everything without moving.
And I… I let it happen.
We broke everything cleanly.

He smiles, a slightly lopsided smile, half in the clouds.

**The father:**
But I don't blame you anymore.
Because you are there.
You held on.
Each in its own, shaky way, but you held on.

He searched for his words for a moment, as if they were floating a little too far away.

**The father:**
We have the same wood, you know.
The same.
Even when he breaks down.
Even when it rots at one end.
It's ours.

He reaches out and squeezes the elder's shoulder. Strong. As we check that a beam is still solid.

The eldest says nothing. He leaves his father's hand there for a second too long, then, without a word, he turns to his brother, beckons him to come closer, and gives him his usual big pat on the back. A little too heavy. As always.

The youngest coughs, surprised, and the three start laughing at the same time. A short laugh, a little broken, but true. The father first, his eyes still half-closed. Then the eldest. Then the younger one.

They laugh in front of the window, without saying anything, while the ball outside remains motionless against the fence.

When the laughter subsides, the father keeps his hand for a moment on the eldest's shoulder, and the eldest keeps his for a moment on the younger's shoulder.

The same wood.

And for the first time in a long time, that was enough.


In the evening, in the bedroom, the father goes back to bed. Exhausted, but the smile that won't go away. He thinks of the hand on the elder's shoulder. To that of the eldest, then to that of the youngest. A piece that holds another, which holds another. He closes his eyes.

*It held because I held on. It was me who put the structure straight. As always.*

He falls asleep with this thought, placed on him like a blanket, and does not see that it is made of the same wood as the one that gave way on the construction site.

In the car, the eldest has the phone to his ear before he even starts.

Master Rossi? Yes, it's me again. Look, the situation has changed. My father is better. Much better, even. It's time to move forward, not to hang around. I want us to relaunch the share buyback file tomorrow morning.

*He hangs up, starts up, doesn't notice that he has already, in his head, placed a new beam in the exact place where the old one broke.*

The youngest returns on foot. He thinks back to the scene, almost in spite of himself, as one tidies up a construction site before nightfall.

Dad stayed in the moment. A piece of wood that has just been installed, still hot from the saw, which does not yet know that it will have to carry a weight. My brother is already cutting another one. He never knew how to stay on the same site for more than one evening. Mom left. She watches from afar, she has put a tarpaulin over what she can no longer carry.

He continues walking, almost satisfied with the clarity of his own diagnosis. Three pieces of wood, three ways to bend under load.

Then he tries to ask the same question about himself.

And me?*He finds nothing to answer. He looks at his hands, empty, which brought nothing that evening, as usual since the accident. He keeps walking.*

The question remains in the air, like a board that someone has forgotten to nail.
