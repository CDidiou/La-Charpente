## The Father

**What he wears well**
Warm-hearted. He has a gift for making everyone feel they have a place, without ever having to ask for it. It is a rare and almost invisible gift: no one notices how much work it takes, because with him it looks so natural. He holds the table, literally and symbolically — the table in the house, the Sunday table, the one that stays laid whatever happens. This is not passivity disguised as kindness — it is a form of power that never seeks attention.

He never builds in order to shut himself away; he builds so that others have somewhere to come back to. He is a convivial man, expansive by nature, the sort who can fill a room with his voice and laughter without ever weighing on anyone. His hands know what they are doing: a floorboard that creaks, a wall that is out of true — he puts it right in a second, almost without thinking, with a quiet pleasure in the gesture itself. In truth, he is more skilful than either of his two sons, who inherited everything except that.

The world, to him, has simple codes: something is well made or it isn't; a line is straight or it isn't. He has never cared for the tortuous paths of his younger son, nor for the fast and sometimes destructive routes taken by his elder — what he seeks, before anything else, is harmony.

**What makes him vulnerable**
He grew up trying, from a very young age, to repair a marriage that was not his to repair — his own father, conciliatory but withdrawn within the home, expansive and festive as soon as he stepped outside; his own mother, who held the reins of the household and actively kept the family running. This habit of smoothing things over, softening things, making sure no one feels they are in the way: he does not always know where helping others ends and losing himself begins.

It took one of his sons watching him renovate a house for him to dare say, for the first time, that he too had been afraid of having nothing solid to pass on.

**His wound**
Not being the one who builds great things, in a family that talks about nothing else.
“I was only holding the table,” he says of himself, with a false modesty that conceals a real anxiety: what if holding the table isn't enough?

**What can be irritating about him**
His way of playing down what he does — “I don't hold anything together, I just get on with things” — at precisely the moment someone is trying to thank him, as though he always has to reject the compliment before they've even finished giving it.

And that old tendency to give way too quickly, to swallow a frustration rather than say it at once, only to bring it back up intact thirty years later, with all its bitterness perfectly preserved.

His search for harmony, too, can turn into avoidance: he quickly tires of his younger son's detours and bristles at his elder son's brutality, as though complexity or friction were, by definition, signs that something had been badly made.

## The Mother

**What she wears well**
A decisiveness strong enough to hold together a marriage, a career, two sons, and probably half the people she has ever crossed paths with. She knows how to forge connections quickly and deeply, with a rare intensity — in a classroom, in a care-home corridor, within a team.

She also possesses something rarer still: the ability to question herself openly, in front of her own children, without being crushed by it. She can hear, “You gave me the keys to a house and forbade me to open the door,” without defending herself, and turn it into clarity rather than hurt.

**What makes her vulnerable**
Her strength itself, turned against her: without even realising it, she finds it difficult to leave room for others, to make decisions for them while believing she is protecting them. She opens up connections with astonishing ease, but always from the same side of the balance — the side that heals, never the side that might itself become vulnerable, or worse, be left out of step.

**Her wound**
As a child, having been an issue to be managed rather than a person loved for herself — useful in preventing a marriage from falling apart, rather than chosen unconditionally.
She has spent her life turning that wound into strength, without always seeing that she sometimes continues to pay the price for it elsewhere, in other relationships, other generations.

**What can be irritating about her**
Her habit of making the decision before the question has even been fully asked, of arriving on a building site and reshuffling everyone's roles within thirty seconds without consulting anyone.
And that way, even now, of closing a subject a little too quickly as soon as she feels it is settled for her — even when, for the person opposite, it isn't quite settled yet.

## The Eldest Son

**What he wears well**
Momentum. He has no fear of starting things: a business, a team, a relationship — he charges ahead, and people follow. He inherited from his mother that ability to set things in motion around him, and turned it into a career as an entrepreneur — tech, finance: modern territories to conquer rather than materials to shape.

He is capable with his hands when the stakes are high enough — a wall to straighten, a steak never to get wrong, thirty years without a single slip — but it is never the gesture itself that feeds him; it is the victory it represents. Unlike his father, who remained rooted in the tangible world of a generation that worked the land and the wood, he prefers challenges played out on a screen, in a market, on a stage — less earth beneath his fingernails, more territory to dominate.

And, rarer in a temperament like his, he can admit when he is wrong — to his father, his mother, his brother — without it diminishing him.

**What makes him vulnerable**
He has built his entire life in reaction to the image of a father who was too conciliatory, and now, having become a father himself, discovers that his own sons do not all follow him with the same enthusiasm — what he calls, with a touch of sad irony, “Dad syndrome”.

Without meaning to, he passes on an anger born of an old sense of betrayal, over trifles that aren't really trifles at all.

**His wound**
The fear that he was right against his father for the wrong reasons — that he chose speed and firmness as a response to a childhood he may have misunderstood when he was a child himself.

**What can be irritating about him**
His impatience, his habit of deciding for others before asking what they think — exactly the thing he sometimes reproaches his mother for, without always seeing himself doing it at precisely the same moment.

And his tendency to look down on his brother's slowness, even affectionately, as though taking one's time were a weakness rather than another kind of strength.

## The Youngest Son

**What he wears well**
A rare subtlety of perception, inherited directly from his mother: in a meeting, a family, a sentence, he sees what no one else sees. He can name with almost surgical precision what others feel but cannot put into words — “You gave me the keys to a house and forbade me to open the door” is a sentence only he could have found.

**What makes him vulnerable**
He grew up overprotected, cherished like the daughter his mother would have liked to have had, and because of that never had the chance to develop the muscles you gain by knocking against the world. He grew up primarily as the object of his mother's care, rather than as her equal — loved, protected, but rarely encountered as the person he actually was.

From this, without meaning to, he learned a quiet lesson: that closeness always comes with control, from one side or the other.

So he prefers to observe relationships before risking himself in them — to remain the one who understands, rather than the one who might in turn be handled like a case. It makes him brilliant at reading people, and excessively cautious about becoming close to them.

**His wound**
Never having been allowed to exercise, for himself, the gift that was nevertheless passed on to him in its entirety — decoding, intuition, psychology.
He took the knowledge without the courage, and he knows it, and it continues to trouble him.

**What can be irritating about him**
His tendency to intellectualise everything, including his own emotions, including the moments when he should simply feel without commenting.

And his slowness to make decisions, which from the outside can look like indecision when, for him, it is a genuine method — but a method that tries the patience of those who love him and who sometimes wait too long for him to take the plunge.
