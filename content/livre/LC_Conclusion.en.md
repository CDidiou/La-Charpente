I am going to try, just once, to explain what I was trying to do with these four. Not because a book needs explaining, but because this one, I think, helped me understand something that the war and coming home had never given me time to understand.

Taken one by one, these four portraits tell four fairly clear stories of circumstance. The father repairs things because he grew up having to mend what was never his to mend. The mother commands and protects because she grew up as something to be dealt with rather than as a person who was simply loved. The eldest charges ahead because one of the two sons had to take the place the other left empty. The youngest watches and doubts, for the same reason, in reverse. None of the four chose the place they started from.

I know that sentence better than anyone: I did not choose mine either, the youngest of a family too large for the land, leaving before I had time to know whether I would have stayed.

And yet none of these four characters, on their own, could ever have turned themselves around. That, I think, is the heart of what I wanted to write — because it is precisely what I was missing. In war, there are plenty of you, packed in on top of one another, and yet you are alone as you never are anywhere else: each man in his own hole, with his own fear, which no one else can carry for him.

You come home convinced that you must face everything alone, because that is what you have just spent months doing. At least, that is what I took from it, and it cost me dearly. The youngest son in this book only allows himself to use his way of seeing in front of his mother, who gives him permission to do so. The father makes peace with the cousins he lost because of his sons, and then because of his wife, who finally tells him everything.

The eldest only understands his own stubbornness while working with a tool beside his father, in a house neither of them could have restored alone. Through them, I wanted to write what I never knew how to do: repair yourself together, rather than come home from a trench believing that solitude is a virtue.

That is what gives the image of the table being held its precise meaning. The father in this book is not the least determined of the four — perhaps he is the one most imprisoned by his role, the man who has been repairing things since childhood without ever truly choosing that part. But it is precisely because he stays in that fixed position, never abandoning it, that the other three have had a stable place from which to dare to move. I never had that table.

I came home to a house where everyone was tearing each other apart over a boundary line, an inheritance, an old grievance that no one could any longer tell in the right order — and there was no one there to keep anything steady while the others, around them, might have learned to move without losing themselves. So I wrote the table I never had.

A table that never moves is not a lack of ambition: it is the infrastructure that allows everything else in a family to move without getting lost. I wanted that table to be solid, as I would have wanted, when I came home, to find someone who had not moved while I was away fighting for a dream that itself slipped away.

True freedom does not live at the bottom of the individual — it lives in the quality of the bond. The family I wanted was one with the capacity, conversation after conversation, to tell its stories to one another, to say aloud what each person had been carrying alone until then. Perhaps it is naïve to write that at my age, after what I have seen. But I think it is precisely because I never lived it that I needed to write it down so carefully.

Nothing in the journey I have invented here claims that an individual can one day free himself, alone, from everything that came before. What the journey tells is something more modest, and truer than what I lived myself: that the only place where circumstance can, here and there, give a little ground is the space between two people who agree to talk to one another, not the space inside a single person. I never had that space.

I came home with one leg missing, and a silence that no one ever came to break for me.

That is why the final image of this book is neither a man alone nor a woman alone who has finally unravelled the mystery of herself, but a table, with four chairs, where each remains what they are — the strategist, the lioness, the builder, the observer — and where the only form of freedom this family will ever truly know comes precisely from remaining seated together, year after year, despite everything that determines each of them separately.

I never had that table.

But I wrote it, as solidly as I could, for someone else.
